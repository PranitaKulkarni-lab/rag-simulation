How RAG works — by actually running it

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. In plain English: before an AI answers your question, it first looks up the most relevant pages from your own documents, then writes its answer using those pages — instead of answering from memory alone and risking making things up.

Below is a working miniature of a real RAG system. Every stage is live: change a slider, edit a document, or ask a different question, and watch the whole pipeline react. The maths here is simplified (real systems use bigger numbers), but every step is the real step, in the real order.

1

Documents — the knowledge the AI is allowed to use

Every RAG system starts with source material: manuals, policies, help pages, notes. The AI can only ever answer from what is in here. Pick a document set, read the documents — and try editing one (change a number!) to see later stages change.

Why it matters: a normal AI chatbot only knows what it learned during training, which may be old or generic. RAG exists to give it your facts — current, private, and checkable.
What can go wrong here: if a fact is not in the documents, RAG cannot invent it honestly — the answer should say “I don’t know.” Out-of-date documents also mean out-of-date answers. Garbage in, garbage out.
2

Chunking — cutting documents into bite-size pieces

Documents are too long to hand to the AI whole, so ahead of time (once, not per question) each document is cut into small pieces called chunks. Drag the sliders and watch the text re-cut itself live. The highlighted start of each chunk is the overlap — text repeated from the previous chunk so a sentence cut in half still makes sense in at least one chunk.

What can go wrong: chunks too big bury the one useful sentence in noise and waste space in the prompt. Chunks too small lose context (“…it costs $75” — what costs $75?). Overlap too low loses meaning at the cuts; too high stores the same text many times.
3

Embedding — turning meaning into numbers

Each chunk is fed to a special model called an embedding model, which converts the chunk’s meaning into a list of numbers called a vector. Chunks that mean similar things get similar numbers — that is the whole trick that makes smart search possible.

Below, each chunk shows the first 8 numbers of its vector. Real vectors have hundreds or thousands of numbers (a common size is 1,536) — far too many to print, so this simulator shows a shortened version and plots just 2 of the numbers on the map.

The meaning-space map

Imagine every chunk placed as a dot on a map where distance = difference in meaning. Dots near each other talk about similar things. Colours = the document each chunk came from. When you ask a question in Stage 5, its dot will land on this same map.

What can go wrong: the embedding model decides what “similar” means. A weak model may park “sick leave” next to “feeling sick about work,” and retrieval later can only find what this map got right.
4

The index — a searchable store of chunks + vectors

All chunks and their vectors are saved together in a vector database (also called the index or vector store). Think of it as a library catalogue where books are shelved by meaning, so “find me things like this question” is a fast lookup instead of reading every page.

Done once, ahead of time: Stages 1–4 happen before anyone asks anything. That is why RAG answers feel instant — the searching library was built in advance. Only Stages 5–8 happen per question.

5

Your question — embedded into the same map

Type a question, or tap a preset. Your question is run through the same embedding model, so it becomes a vector too and lands as a ★ star on the meaning-space map (scroll up to Stage 3 to see it, or watch the mini-map in Stage 6). Retrieval is now just: “which chunk dots are closest to the star?”

Its vector:

What can go wrong: vague questions land in vague places on the map. “Tell me about the thing” has no strong meaning signal, so retrieval guesses. Specific words from the documents (“parental leave”, “meal allowance”) pull the star to the right neighbourhood.
6

Retrieval — fetching the closest chunks

The system scores every chunk against your question. The score is a similarity score from 0 to 1 — how close the chunk’s vector sits to the question’s vector (this is usually measured with cosine similarity: 1 = same direction/meaning, 0 = unrelated). It then keeps the best few. You control how many, and how good is good enough.

What can go wrong: Top K too high drags in off-topic chunks that confuse the answer (“noise”). Top K too low or a threshold too strict can miss the one chunk with the answer. There is no perfect setting — it is a tuning trade-off, and now you can feel it.
7

Prompt assembly — building the augmented prompt

This is the “augmented” part of the name. Nobody sends your bare question to the AI. The system quietly builds a bigger prompt: an instruction, plus the retrieved chunks as evidence, plus your question. This exact text (live, updating as you change anything above) is what the language model actually receives.


  
Prompt length: 0 charactersReal models limit this — the context window. It is one reason chunk size and Top K matter: everything retrieved has to fit.

  
What can go wrong: if retrieval fetched the wrong chunks, this prompt confidently hands the AI the wrong evidence. The model can only be as grounded as what it is given here.







  
8

    
Generation — the answer, written from the evidence

    
The language model (the LLM — the same kind of AI as a chatbot) reads the assembled prompt and writes the final answer. A well-behaved RAG answer sticks to the chunks and shows its sources, so you can check it. Press the button — in this simulator the “model” picks the most relevant sentences from the retrieved chunks, which is a fair miniature of what grounding means.

The one-sentence summary to remember: RAG = search first, then write. The LLM supplies the language skills; your documents supply the facts.

  

    Recap — the pipeline in 10 seconds
    
Once, ahead of time: Documents → cut into chunks (with overlap) → each chunk embedded into a vector → vectors stored in an index.

    Per question: question embedded → retrieve Top K closest chunks above the threshold → assemble prompt (instruction + chunks + question) → LLM generates a grounded, cited answer.
